The question

Where and when do crashes happen in New York, and where and when are they more serious? An actuary separates frequency (how often crashes happen) from severity (how bad they are when they do), so this page shows both. Every figure is computed in your browser from the processed data files.

    How to read this, and what it leaves out

    What a "crash" is here

    Each row in the DMV file is one crash case, not one person or one vehicle. The file covers … to …, the only window the DMV publishes in this form, so the month and hour patterns are …-year totals. Severity is the DMV's own four-level descriptor: Fatal, Injury, Property Damage & Injury, and Property Damage. I kept those levels as published. "Injury or fatal" is my grouping of the first three levels, because the mixed "Property Damage & Injury" level does not say how serious the injury was.

    Frequency, severity and exposure

    In insurance terms, frequency is how often a loss happens and severity is how bad it is when it does. Both depend on exposure, the amount of risk-taking that opens people to the loss. The best exposure measure for driving is vehicle miles travelled (VMT). I did not find a county-level VMT table on data.ny.gov that I could verify, so I used county population (NYS Department of Labor estimates, averaged over … to …) as a rougher stand-in. That is why the county chart shows crashes per 1,000 residents per year and not crashes per mile.

    Population is a weak substitute. Residents of rural counties tend to drive more miles per person, and visitors and through traffic are counted in crashes but not in population. … County, with about … residents, tops the list at … crashes per 1,000 residents per year, and its … crashes include many that involve people who do not live there. Treat the ranking as a description of crashes relative to residents and not as a ranking of how risky each county's drivers are.

    The reporting threshold problem

    The crash file does not capture every crash. Minor property-damage crashes are reported only above a damage threshold, and many are never reported at all, while injury and fatal crashes almost always are. That makes the mix of outcomes depend on how much minor damage gets recorded. The five New York City counties show it clearly: together they hold …% of the state's population but …% of its recorded crashes, and their injury-or-fatal shares run from …% (…) to …% (…) against a county median of …%. They also have the lowest crash frequencies in the state (… per 1,000 residents in … County). The likeliest reading is that fewer minor crashes reach the file there, which would push the injury share up and the frequency down. The data cannot prove that, but it means a high injury share in this file should not be read as more dangerous driving.

    Fatal crashes are the most complete measure, so the fatal rate per 100,000 residents is the steadiest comparison between counties. Even that is noisy for small counties: … of the … counties had fewer than … fatal crashes in … years and are flagged in the table, where one extra crash moves the rate noticeably. This is the same small-numbers idea actuaries call credibility: a small county's experience alone says little about its underlying risk.

    Time of day and data quality

    Hour 0 (midnight to 12:59 am) holds … crashes, … times the … in hour 1, and its fatal rate per 1,000 crashes (…) is …% of hour 1's (…). That pattern fits a default midnight time for crashes whose time is unknown, mixing ordinary daytime crashes into that hour. Read hour 0 with care. The hour-by-day heatmap has no exposure either, since it shows how many crashes occurred, not how many cars were on the road, so rush hours are busy because roads are busy. Weather, road surface and light carry the same limit: most driving happens on dry roads in daylight, and their "Unknown" rows have almost no fatal crashes, which points to fields left blank on minor crashes.

    What this page does not show

    It does not show the cause of any crash, the fault of any driver, or the chance that a given trip ends in a crash. It uses crashes as recorded, with no adjustment for under-reporting, and compares counties with no adjustment for road type, climate or traffic mix. It is best used to ask where to look more closely, for example by pairing the data with VMT if that becomes available. The … crashes with an unknown county are left out of the county table only.
