The question

In spring 2020 unemployment jumped almost everywhere. How big was the jump in each part of New York, and how long did it take each county, metro area and region to get back to where it was before? Every figure below is computed in your browser from the processed data files, so none is typed in by hand.

    The 2020 shock, statewide

    A line chart suits a rate that moves month to month: the spike and the slow descent are the story. The series is not seasonally adjusted, so small seasonal wiggles are normal.

    Regional comparison

    New York State's Department of Labor groups counties into ten labor market regions. Pick a region to compare its monthly rate with the state. Use Show to switch between the rate itself and its gap to the same month of 2019, which is the comparison the recovery times on this page are built on.

    The gap view subtracts the rate in the same calendar month of 2019 from every month. That takes the seasonal pattern out, so 2019 is a flat zero by construction, and anything above zero is unemployment over and above what that time of year looked like before the shock. The dotted line is this page's tolerance (): a place counts as recovered in the first month after its peak when its line is on or under it, and larger dots mark the region's peak and that month. The gap at the peak is measured against the same month of 2019, so it differs from the "jump" in the table below, which is measured from February 2020.

    One small chart per county, all on the same vertical scale (0% to %) so they can be compared by eye. The shaded band marks the spring 2020 peak window. Sort to see who was hit hardest or recovered last. Press any county to see it up close.

      Small multiples repeat one simple chart so the eye compares shapes rather than decoding a legend. Each chart shows January 2018 to the latest month; the gap near late 2025 is the month missing from the source.

      One county, up close

      Pick a county here, or press a small chart or a county name in the table above. The dotted recovery line is the county's own rate in the same month of 2019 plus point, drawn from the peak onward. The county counts as recovered in the first month after its peak when its rate is on or under that line, so you can see the definition at work instead of taking the number on trust.

      What if the rule were stricter or looser?

      Every recovery time on this page rests on one choice: a place counts as recovered when its rate is within of the same month of 2019. Move the slider to change that tolerance and see how much the answer depends on it. Left is stricter (at 0.0 the rate must be at or below its 2019 level), right is looser. Nothing else on the page changes; this is a test of the rule, recomputed in your browser from the monthly rates.

      A cumulative step chart suits a "how long until" question: each step up is one or more counties reaching the line, and a curve that sits further left means a faster recovery. Two curves on one scale show the whole spread of counties moving, not just an average.

      How to read this, and what it leaves out

      The unemployment rate is the share of the labor force (people working or actively looking for work) who are looking and have no job. It does not count people who stopped looking, so a falling rate can partly reflect people leaving the labor force. County figures are model-based estimates, and small counties are noisier than large ones.
